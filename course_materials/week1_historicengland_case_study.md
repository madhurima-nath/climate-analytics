# Week 1 Case Study: Historic England
## Climate Intelligence — University of Reading (FutureLearn)

Source: Audio interview with Dr. Hannah Fluck, Head of Environmental Strategy, Historic England.
Historic England provides advice and support to the heritage sector on looking after and maintaining heritage assets: archaeological sites, buildings, parks and gardens, coastal wrecks, and designed landscapes.

---

### What aspects of weather data are most relevant to Historic England's clients?

The transcript focuses on hazard outcomes rather than raw meteorological variables, but three weather phenomena come through clearly:

**Precipitation intensity.** More intense rainfall is already causing visible problems: gutters and rainwater systems failing, localised flash flooding, and water penetrating the external fabric of older buildings in ways it had not done previously.

**Temperature.** Rising temperatures drive overheating of buildings and landscapes. Temperature changes also cause shrink-swell behaviour in clay-rich soils, making the ground expand and contract more than many structures were built to tolerate.

**Storms and coastal conditions.** Wave action and coastal erosion affect heritage along England's coastlines, with cliff stability playing a role alongside wave energy.

The counterintuitive finding: in workshops, people name storms, coastal erosion, and flooding as the top hazards. When Historic England compared the National Heritage List against hazard maps, the highest exposure was to overheating. Around 73% of designated heritage assets in England will be exposed to the highest overheating levels by 2060. Currently none are.

---

### Which aspects of the changing climate are most relevant to Historic England's clients?

Three separate problems:

**Overheating.** The most significant and most surprising hazard. The shift between current exposure (none at the highest overheating level) and projected exposure by 2060 (around three quarters of all designated assets) is large. The complication: what overheating actually means for archaeological sites, historic landscapes, and green heritage is not yet understood. The direction of change is clear; the impact on specific asset types often is not.

**Intense precipitation.** More intense rainfall events are causing structural damage to building fabric, flash flooding, and rainwater system failures. Already happening and expected to worsen.

**Ground instability.** Driven by two mechanisms: shrink-swell in clay soils from temperature changes, and accelerated erosion and landslip from both rainfall and temperature. Coastal cliff instability compounds this for heritage near the coast. Each can damage or destroy assets that cannot be replaced.

One point from the transcript worth noting: Hannah Fluck is candid that the heritage sector is "making it up as we go along." The connection between known climate hazards and actual impacts on specific heritage assets is still being worked out. There is no established playbook.

---

### What timescales are of interest? Hours, days, weeks, months, seasons, years, decades or multiple decades?

No short-term timescales appear in the transcript. The relevant timescales are:

**Decades (near-term: next 20-30 years).** The practical planning horizon for most organisations. Over this period, the difference between emissions scenarios is relatively small, so understanding the direction of change is enough to take action. Hannah Fluck describes trend awareness as sufficient to act.

**To 2060.** The specific horizon cited for the overheating projections.

**Century-scale.** Heritage assets have existed for hundreds or thousands of years and the goal is to preserve them for hundreds or thousands more. At this timescale, different emissions scenarios diverge significantly. The recommendation is to use high emissions (worst case) projections because heritage is irreplaceable and the cost of getting it wrong cannot be undone.

---

### Is the information they need highly localised in time or space, or is a broader picture of large-scale geographical trends sufficient?

Both, but in sequence.

A broad overview comes first. Historic England developed hazard mapping at a 5km grid across England, drawing on publicly available data from the Met Office, Ordnance Survey, BGS, and the Environment Agency. This gives a national picture of which hazards matter and where, identifying priority risks and knowledge gaps. Hannah Fluck is explicit that this is a coarse overview, designed to indicate whether a site needs further attention, not to drive specific decisions directly.

Localised information follows once a particular hazard has been flagged for a specific asset. Climate change is not uniform across England; which aspects are changing and by how much varies by region. Taking action on a specific site requires understanding local conditions and local vulnerability.

The data itself is described as a "minefield": substantial and publicly available, but variable in quality and age, requiring specialist expertise to navigate and combine effectively.

---

### Do they need access to detailed quantitative numerical data to feed into models of their business activities, or is summarised information sufficient (e.g., a graph or a plot)?

Hannah Fluck's closing statement answers this directly: "don't get overly concerned about the details unless you know why you need to... it's more important to be thinking about change and the direction and the trends."

For most of Historic England's clients (building owners, local authorities, heritage managers, conservation organisations), the primary need is for accessible, summarised information: hazard maps, overviews of what is changing and where, and trend directions that allow people to make decisions without being climate scientists.

Detailed quantitative projections become relevant only once a specific hazard has been identified for a specific site and a decision requires more precision. Even then, the advice is to use high emissions scenarios rather than searching for the most accurate projection, because the cost of underestimating risk is too high when the assets involved cannot be replaced.

This stands in contrast to EnergyMetric, whose clients need precise numerical data fed directly into generation models. Historic England's clients need to understand what to prioritise and why, which calls for accessible summaries first.
